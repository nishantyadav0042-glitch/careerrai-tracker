## The 10 new items

### ⚡ Shortcuts (3) — the highest "I'm keeping this" value

**N1. The flip**
What is 8% of 25?

- A) 4 · **B) 2** · C) 2.5 · D) 20

→ **2.** Because *a% of b = b% of a*, and 25% of 8 is obviously 2. This works
every single time and almost nobody is taught it.
*Share reason: the most genuinely delightful trick in either batch. People send
things that feel like a secret.*

**N2. Times five**
86 × 5, in your head, in three seconds.

- A) 420 · **B) 430** · C) 440 · D) 415

→ **430.** Multiplying by 5 is halving and adding a zero: 860 ÷ 2. Works for any
number, instantly.
*Share reason: immediately usable, and the method is easier to explain than the
answer.*

**N3. Nearly squares**
51 × 49 without multiplying out.

- A) 2,501 · **B) 2,499** · C) 2,500 · D) 2,449

→ **2,499.** It's (50+1)(50−1) = 50² − 1. Any pair equally spaced around a round
number collapses to a square minus a square.
*Share reason: the answer being one away from 2,500 is the satisfying part.*

### 🛒 Everyday life (3) — CAT thinking hidden inside an ordinary decision

You singled B15 out for exactly this. These extend it.

**N4. Which order?**
A ₹500 shirt. The shop has 10% off **and** a "₹50 off above ₹400" coupon. They
apply in whichever order you ask.

- **A) Percentage first** · B) Coupon first · C) Same either way · D) Depends

→ **Percentage first.** 500 → 450 → **₹400**. The other way: 500 → 450 → 405.
Percentages should always be applied while the amount is still large.
*Share reason: it's real money, and the ₹5 gap is small enough to feel like a
secret rather than a maths problem.*

**N5. Two good years**
Your salary rises 10% one year and 10% the next. Total rise?

- A) 20% · **B) 21%** · C) 19% · D) 22%

→ **21%.** The second 10% is taken on the larger amount. Compounding quietly
works in your favour here — and quietly against you on a loan.
*Share reason: everyone with a job wants this to be true, and it is.*

**N6. Which offer?**
Two shops, same shirt, same price.

- Shop A: buy 3, get 1 free
- **Shop B: 30% off**

→ **Shop B.** Buy-3-get-1 means paying for 3 of 4 — a 25% discount. "Free"
reads bigger than it is, which is precisely why shops use it.
*Share reason: changes how someone shops tomorrow, which is the strongest
possible reason to forward something.*

### 🕵️ Spot it (2)

**N7. Does it follow?**
All athletes are disciplined. Some disciplined people are early risers.
Therefore, some athletes are early risers.

- A) Follows · **B) Doesn't follow** · C) Only if all athletes rise early · D) Can't say

→ **Doesn't follow.** The "some disciplined people" who rise early might be
entirely non-athletes. Two statements can both be true and still connect to
nothing.
*Share reason: feels like a brain-teaser, not a syllabus item, and people enjoy
being right about it.*

**N8. Premise or conclusion?**
*"The road was wet, so it must have rained."*

Which part is the conclusion?

- A) The road was wet
- **B) It must have rained**
- C) Both · D) Neither

→ **B.** "So" points at the conclusion. The wet road is the evidence — and note
it's weak evidence, since a burst pipe would do the same. Spotting which half is
being *claimed* is most of critical reasoning.
*Share reason: short, clean, and the burst-pipe aside is the bit people repeat.*

### 🧠 Gotcha, without percentages (2)

**N9. Two classes**
Class A averages 80. Class B averages 90. Combined average?

- A) 85 · B) 87.5 · **C) Not enough information** · D) 84

→ **Not enough information.** It depends entirely on how many students are in
each class. 10 students at 80 and 90 at 90 gives 89, not 85. Averages of
averages are almost never the average.
*Share reason: the "obviously 85" instinct is universal, and the correction is
useful far beyond CAT.*

**N10. Seven or eight?**
Roll two dice. Which total is more likely?

- **A) 7** · B) 8 · C) Equally likely · D) Depends on the dice

→ **7.** There are six ways to make 7 (1-6, 2-5, 3-4 and their reverses) and only
five ways to make 8. Seven is the most likely total on two dice, which is why
it's the number every dice game is built around.
*Share reason: settles an argument people genuinely have, with a countable proof.*

# Today's Takeaway — 30 daily tips

The second half of the daily object. One line under the question, 12–18 words,
saveable and sendable.

**The rule these are written to:** a tip must produce *"oh, I do that"*, not
*"yes, obviously"*. Anything that could appear on a motivational poster is
excluded — "consistency is key" and "practice makes perfect" are dead on
arrival, because the student has seen them ten thousand times.

**These are CareerRai-curated, from established CAT strategy.** They are NOT
labelled as data-driven, because we don't have that evidence yet. When we do,
they become "CareerRai Insight" and say so. Keeping those two apart is what
makes the second one believable later.

1. If you're rereading an RC line twice, stop reading harder. Ask what the author is proving.
2. Don't spend three minutes proving a question is solvable. Spend twenty seconds deciding if it's worth solving.
3. If your QA solution gets longer every line, you missed the observation. Start again, don't push.
4. In DILR, the fastest set isn't the easiest one. It's the one with the cleanest entry.
5. A mock you didn't analyse wasn't a mock. It was three hours of expensive guessing.
6. The topic you keep postponing is your actual weakness. Everything else is a preference.
7. Revision isn't rereading. If you're not recalling with the page shut, you're just visiting.
8. You don't have a time problem in QA. You have a which-question problem.
9. Five hours on comfortable topics is not five hours of preparation.
10. If you can eliminate two options, guess. If you can't eliminate any, walk away.
11. Your first read of a passage decides your accuracy. Everything after it is damage control.
12. An attempt rate below 60% is usually fear, not strategy.
13. Studying after a bad mock feels productive. Reading that mock is what actually helps.
14. The set you understand slowly beats the set you might understand quickly.
15. Consistency isn't studying daily. It's coming back the day after you missed one.
16. You remember what you struggled to recall, never what you highlighted.
17. If a formula needs looking up twice in a week, it isn't learned yet.
18. Skipping a question is a decision. Running out of time is not.
19. Percentages don't cancel out. Neither do the days you planned to make up later.
20. Two mocks a week without analysis is worse than one mock with it.
21. If you've solved it correctly three times, stop. That's practice you no longer need.
22. The last ten minutes of a section decide more marks than the first ten.
23. You can't fix accuracy and speed in the same week. Pick one.
24. Reading the solution is not solving. Close it and rebuild it from memory.
25. The question you left isn't what cost you. It's the one you didn't leave.
26. Everyone's plan survives until the first bad week. That week is the plan.
27. A topic feels easy right after you study it. Test it four days later instead.
28. If your notes are beautiful, your energy went somewhere it wasn't needed.
29. Don't compare your day 40 with someone else's day 200.
30. Motivation starts you. A plan you can do on a bad day is what keeps you here.
