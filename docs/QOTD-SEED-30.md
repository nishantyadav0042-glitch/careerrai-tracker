## The brief these were written to

Not a question bank. Thirty deliberate experiences for a student who has studied
nothing today and is deciding whether to open the app tomorrow.

- **Understandable in under 15 seconds.** No long setups.
- **Solvable in 60–90 seconds.** If it needs scratch paper for three minutes, it
  belongs in a mock, not here.
- **One trap or one insight each.** The feeling to produce is *"I almost fell for
  that"* or *"that's clever"* — never *"I don't know this."*
- **The explanation is the product.** The answer is just the receipt.
- **No difficulty labels shown to students.** Internal only; the moment a card
  says "Hard", it becomes an assessment and the tired student closes it.

Mix: QA 12 · VARC 10 · DILR 8. Served in shuffled order, never one section for a
week, so the student cannot predict what's coming.

**Where I need you most:** the VARC items (17–26). Their answers are defensible
but verbal is where a "technically correct" answer annoys a good student most.
The QA arithmetic I have checked; the VARC judgement is yours.

## QA — familiar entry (confidence builders)

### 1. Successive discounts
A jacket is marked ₹2,000. The shop offers 20% off, then another 10% off the
reduced price. What is the final price?

- A) ₹1,400 · **B) ₹1,440** · C) ₹1,460 · D) ₹1,500

**Trap:** reading 20% + 10% as 30% off (₹1,400).
**Explanation:** Discounts compound, they don't add. 2000 × 0.8 = 1600, then
1600 × 0.9 = **1440**. The two discounts apply to different bases — the second
one is smaller in rupees than it looks.
*Topic: percentages · Trap: additive-vs-multiplicative · ~45s*

### 2. The replaced number
The average of 10 numbers is 25. One of them, 30, is replaced by another number
and the average becomes 24. What is the new number?

- A) 15 · **B) 20** · C) 24 · D) 29

**Explanation:** The total fell by 10 × 1 = 10. So the new number is 30 − 10 =
**20**. You never need the other nine numbers — averages questions are usually
about the *total*, not the list.
*Topic: averages · Insight: work with the sum · ~40s*

### 3. Working together
A finishes a job in 12 days, B in 18. Working together, how long?

- A) 6 · B) 6.5 · **C) 7.2** · D) 15

**Explanation:** Add rates, not days. 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36, so 36/5 = **7.2 days**.
*Topic: time & work · Trap: averaging 12 and 18 · ~50s*

### 4. Splitting the money
₹1,800 is split between A, B and C in the ratio 2 : 3 : 4. How much more does C
get than A?

- A) ₹200 · B) ₹300 · **C) ₹400** · D) ₹600

**Explanation:** 9 parts, so one part = 200. C − A = 2 parts = **₹400**. Answer
the question asked — the difference, not C's share (₹800).
*Topic: ratios · Trap: solving for the wrong quantity · ~40s*

### 5. Two sales
An item costing ₹500 is sold at 20% profit. The buyer resells it at 10% loss.
What did the second buyer pay?

- A) ₹500 · B) ₹530 · **C) ₹540** · D) ₹550

**Explanation:** 500 × 1.2 = 600; 600 × 0.9 = **540**. Note it isn't 550 — the
10% loss is taken on 600, not on 500.
*Topic: profit & loss · Trap: shifting base · ~45s*

## QA — the clever shortcut (satisfaction)

### 6. There and back
You drive 60 km at 30 km/h and return the same 60 km at 60 km/h. Average speed
for the whole trip?

- A) 45 km/h · **B) 40 km/h** · C) 42 km/h · D) 48 km/h

**Trap:** averaging 30 and 60.
**Explanation:** Average speed = total distance / total time. 120 km in
(2 + 1) = 3 hours = **40 km/h**. You spend twice as long at the slow speed, so
the slow speed counts twice as much. This one shows up in CAT in a dozen
costumes.
*Topic: speed · Trap: arithmetic mean of rates · ~60s*

### 7. Up then down
A price rises 20%, then falls 20%. Compared with the start, it is now:

- A) unchanged · **B) 4% lower** · C) 4% higher · D) 2% lower

**Explanation:** 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96 → **4% lower**. Any up-x%-then-down-x% always
loses, by x²/100 percent. Worth memorising as a shape, not a formula.
*Topic: percentages · Trap: symmetry that isn't · ~40s*

### 8. Last digit
What is the last digit of 7^35?

- A) 1 · B) 9 · C) 7 · **D) 3**

**Explanation:** Last digits of powers of 7 cycle 7, 9, 3, 1 with period 4.
35 mod 4 = 3, so the third in the cycle: **3**. No computation, just the cycle.
*Topic: number system · Insight: cyclicity · ~60s*

### 9. Don't expand
If x + 1/x = 4, what is x² + 1/x²?

- A) 12 · B) 15 · **C) 14** · D) 16

**Explanation:** Square both sides: x² + 2 + 1/x² = 16, so **14**. Solving for x
itself is possible and completely unnecessary — CAT algebra usually rewards
manipulating the expression rather than finding the variable.
*Topic: algebra · Insight: don't solve for x · ~55s*

## QA — the trap

### 10. The discount that isn't
A shopkeeper marks goods 40% above cost, then offers a 25% discount. His profit
percentage is:

- A) 15% · **B) 5%** · C) 10% · D) he loses 5%

**Explanation:** 1.40 × 0.75 = 1.05 → **5% profit**. Mark-up and discount work on
different bases, which is exactly why "40% up, 25% off" still leaves a profit.
*Topic: profit & loss · Trap: 40 − 25 = 15 · ~55s*

### 11. Half the work
A can do a job in 10 days. He works for 4 days and leaves. B finishes the rest in
9 days. How long would B alone take for the whole job?

- A) 12 · B) 13.5 · **C) 15** · D) 18

**Explanation:** A did 4/10 = 2/5. B did 3/5 in 9 days, so the whole job takes
9 × 5/3 = **15 days**.
*Topic: time & work · Trap: 9 + 4 or 9 × 10/4 · ~70s*

### 12. Which is bigger?
Without calculating: which is larger, 31/47 or 33/50?

- **A) 33/50** · B) 31/47 · C) equal · D) cannot be determined

**Explanation:** 31/47 ≈ 0.6596, 33/50 = 0.66 — but you don't need decimals.
Cross-multiply: 31 × 50 = 1550 versus 33 × 47 = 1551. **33/50** wins, by a hair.
The insight is that "obviously bigger" fractions are often nearly equal, and
cross-multiplication settles it in seconds.
*Topic: fractions · Insight: cross-multiply · ~50s*

## VARC — inference and tone

### 13. What follows
*"The company's revenue grew every quarter for three years. Its share price fell
in each of those quarters."*

Which is best supported?

- A) The revenue figures were false.
- **B) Something other than revenue was driving the share price.**
- C) The company was badly managed.
- D) Investors behave irrationally.

**Explanation:** **B** is the only option that stays inside the passage. A, C and
D each import an explanation the two sentences do not supply. CAT inference
questions punish the interesting answer and reward the boring one.
*Skill: inference discipline · Trap: the plausible story · ~60s*

### 14. The author's attitude
*"Naturally, the committee spent four months deciding the colour of the folder in
which the report would be bound."*

The author's tone is best described as:

- A) neutral · **B) ironic** · C) angry · D) admiring

**Explanation:** "Naturally" signals the opposite of natural, and the mismatch
between four months and a folder colour does the rest. **Ironic**, not angry —
there is no outrage in the sentence, only a raised eyebrow. Tone questions are
usually decided by one word.
*Skill: tone · Insight: find the signal word · ~45s*

### 15. Weaken it
*"Cities with more parks have healthier residents. Therefore building parks
improves public health."*

Which most weakens the argument?

- A) Some park visitors are unhealthy.
- **B) Wealthier neighbourhoods tend to have both more parks and better healthcare.**
- C) Parks are expensive to maintain.
- D) Not everyone uses parks.

**Explanation:** **B** offers a common cause for both facts, which is the classic
way a correlation-to-causation argument breaks. C is irrelevant to the logic; A
and D nibble at the edges without touching the causal claim.
*Skill: causal reasoning · Trap: attacking the data instead of the link · ~70s*

## VARC — summary and structure

### 16. Para-summary
*"Most productivity advice assumes the problem is a shortage of time. For many
people the real shortage is attention: the hours exist, but they arrive in
fragments too small to do anything demanding."*

Best summary:

- A) People should manage their time better.
- **B) The real constraint is often fragmented attention, not a lack of hours.**
- C) Productivity advice is useless.
- D) Demanding work requires long hours.

**Explanation:** **B** carries both halves — the correction *and* what it
corrects. A repeats what the passage argues against; C overstates ("useless");
D is a detail, not the point. A summary that drops the contrast has dropped the
paragraph.
*Skill: summary · Trap: the overstated option · ~65s*

### 17. Odd one out
Which sentence does not belong?

1. Handwriting is disappearing from schools.
2. Typing is faster for most students.
3. My grandmother's letters were beautiful to look at.
4. Several countries have removed cursive from the curriculum.

- A) 1 · B) 2 · **C) 3** · D) 4

**Explanation:** 1, 2 and 4 are claims about a general trend. **3** is a personal
recollection — different register, different scope. Odd-one-out is usually about
the *kind* of statement, not the subject matter.
*Skill: coherence · Insight: match register, not topic · ~55s*

### 18. Sentence placement
Where does this sentence belong? *"That assumption is rarely examined."*

*(1) Public transport policy usually starts from the belief that people want to
travel as fast as possible. (2) ___ (3) In several surveys, reliability mattered
more to commuters than speed.*

- A) before 1 · **B) at 2** · C) after 3 · D) it doesn't fit

**Explanation:** "That assumption" must point back at something — sentence 1
supplies it — and sentence 3 supplies the examination. **Position 2** is the
only slot where both halves work. Follow the pronoun.
*Skill: cohesion · Insight: pronouns point backwards · ~60s*

## VARC — elimination as a skill

### 19. Two survive
*"The novel was praised by critics and ignored by readers."*

Which CANNOT be concluded?

- A) Critics liked it.
- B) It sold poorly.
- **C) The critics were wrong.**
- D) Praise and sales don't always align.

**Explanation:** **C** is a judgement the sentence never makes. This question is
really about a habit: on "cannot be concluded", look for the option that adds an
opinion rather than the one that adds a fact.
*Skill: elimination · Trap: agreeing with a reasonable opinion · ~55s*

### 20. The word that decides
*"The policy may, in some circumstances, reduce delays."*

Which option is consistent with the sentence?

- A) The policy reduces delays.
- **B) The policy sometimes might reduce delays.**
- C) The policy has no effect.
- D) The policy always reduces delays.

**Explanation:** "May" and "in some circumstances" are both hedges, and **B** is
the only option that keeps both. In VARC the qualifiers are not decoration —
they are usually the entire question.
*Skill: precision · Insight: hedges are load-bearing · ~40s*

## DILR — micro-sets

### 21. Four friends, one table
P, Q, R, S sit in a row. P is not at either end. Q is immediately right of P.
S is at the left end. Who is at the right end?

- A) P · B) Q · **C) R** · D) cannot be determined

**Explanation:** S is leftmost. P isn't at an end, so P is in position 2 or 3.
Q is immediately right of P. If P is 2, Q is 3, R is 4. If P is 3, Q is 4 — but
then position 2 is R and P is fine… wait: check the ends. In the second case Q
is at the right end, contradicting nothing yet — except S is at position 1 and P
at 3 leaves R at 2, which is consistent. **Founder note: this item has two valid
arrangements and the answer is therefore "cannot be determined". Flagging it
rather than shipping it — see the reject list below.**
*STATUS: REJECTED by my own check. Replacement below as 21b.*

### 21b. Four friends, one table (fixed)
P, Q, R, S sit in a row. S is at the left end. Q is immediately right of P.
R is at the right end. Where is P?

- A) position 1 · **B) position 2** · C) position 3 · D) cannot be determined

**Explanation:** S is 1 and R is 4, so P and Q occupy 2 and 3 — and since Q is
immediately right of P, P is **2**. Lock the fixed positions first; the
constraint then has only one place to go.
*Skill: arrangement · Insight: place the certainties first · ~60s*

### 22b. Reading the table (fixed)
| | Mon | Tue | Wed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 40 | 50 | 45 |
| Returns | 4 | 11 | 9 |

On which day was the return *rate* highest?

- A) Mon · **B) Tue** · C) Wed · D) all equal

**Explanation:** 10%, 22%, 20% → **Tue**. Wednesday has fewer returns than
Tuesday in absolute terms but the question asks for rate, and the base changed.
*Skill: table reading · Trap: comparing absolutes when asked for rates · ~55s*

### 23. One step of deduction
In a group of 40 students, 25 play cricket and 20 play football. Every student
plays at least one. How many play both?

- **A) 5** · B) 10 · C) 15 · D) 45

**Explanation:** 25 + 20 = 45, which exceeds 40 by **5** — that overlap is the
both-count. The whole question is one subtraction once you see the shape.
*Skill: sets · Insight: overcount = overlap · ~45s*

## DILR — deduction

### 24. Who is lying
Three people. Exactly one is telling the truth.
A says: "B is lying." B says: "C is lying." C says: "A and B are both lying."

Who is telling the truth?

- A) A · **B) B** · C) C · D) impossible

**Explanation:** Suppose **B** is truthful. Then C is lying — consistent, since C
claims both A and B lie, which is false because B is truthful. And A must be
lying, so B is truthful — consistent. Test the other two and each collapses.
Truth-teller puzzles are solved by assuming and checking, not by reasoning
forwards.
*Skill: logical deduction · Insight: assume, then test · ~85s*

### 25. Ranking from fragments
Five runners. C finished ahead of A. B finished behind D. A finished ahead of D.
Who could NOT have finished first?

- A) B and C · **B) A, B and D** · C) only B · D) cannot be determined

**Explanation:** C > A > D > B, so C is ahead of all three of A, D and B. Any of
**A, B or D** therefore cannot be first (E is unconstrained and could be).
*Skill: ordering · Insight: chain the constraints · ~75s*

## DILR — set selection (the CAT skill nobody teaches)

### 26. Which set would you open?
You have 12 minutes left and two sets remain.

- **Set A:** 4 questions, 6 conditions, three of which fix exact positions.
- **Set B:** 4 questions, 5 conditions, all of them comparative ("more than",
  "not adjacent to").

Which is the better opening?

- **A) Set A** · B) Set B · C) either · D) neither, attempt singles

**Explanation:** **Set A.** Conditions that fix exact positions collapse the
possibilities immediately; purely comparative conditions leave many arrangements
alive and cost you minutes before the first answer appears. Count *fixing*
conditions, not total conditions — that's the 30-second read that decides your
DILR score more than your reasoning does.
*Skill: set selection · Insight: fixing beats plentiful · ~60s*

### 27. The expensive set
A set takes you 4 minutes to set up and then yields all 4 answers quickly.
Another yields its first answer in 1 minute but the remaining 3 need separate
work. With 6 minutes left, which is better?

- **A) The second** · B) The first · C) Identical · D) Skip both

**Explanation:** **The second.** With 6 minutes, the first set risks spending 4
minutes and banking nothing if the setup goes wrong. The second guarantees a
mark in 60 seconds. Late in a section, guaranteed marks beat efficient ones.
*Skill: exam judgement · Insight: risk changes with the clock · ~55s*

## The "Would you attempt this?" format (3)

These show the question and ask **Attempt / Skip** *before* revealing anything.
They score nothing. They measure judgement, which is the thing CAT actually
tests and no other app records.

### 28. Attempt or skip?
*"In how many ways can 8 distinct books be arranged on 3 shelves if each shelf
must hold at least one?"*

**Attempt / Skip** → then: **Skip** is the better call inside a timed section
unless you have seen this exact form before. It's a compositions-plus-
arrangements question that looks like a 40-second problem and is a 3-minute one.
Recognising expensive questions *before* starting is worth more marks than
solving them.

### 29. Attempt or skip?
*"If the average of five consecutive even numbers is 42, what is the largest?"*

**Attempt / Skip** → then: **Attempt.** The middle of five consecutive terms is
the average, so 42 is the third; the largest is 46. Ten seconds. Some questions
look routine and are genuinely free — skipping those is as costly as attempting
the expensive ones.

### 30. Attempt or skip?
*"A 700-word passage on the epistemology of scientific consensus, with 4
questions."*

**Attempt / Skip** → then: **It depends on what's left**, and that is the point.
With 20 minutes remaining it's four marks from one read. With 6 minutes it's a
trap that eats the section. The same question is a good and a bad decision at
different times, and students who never think this way lose marks they had the
ability to score.

Two items (21, 22) had **two valid answers** and I caught it on my own check, not
in review. They're left visible above rather than quietly deleted, because the
failure rate matters: roughly 1 in 15 of my first drafts was ambiguous. That is

argument for shipping in batches of ten.

Also deliberately excluded, per the brief: full RC passages (wrong interaction
for a 60-second card), full DILR sets (too much load), formula recall (school,
not CAT), trivia, and anything whose explanation is just the answer restated.
