# 🎯 What would YOU do?

Your signature format. The student answers *before* any calculation, so friction
is near zero — and they can disagree with us, which is the bridge into Student
Insights.

### B1. Four minutes in
You've spent 4 minutes on a QA question. You can feel you're close. There are
2 minutes left in the section and 3 unattempted questions.

**Push on / Move on**

→ **Move on.** The sunk cost is the trap: those 4 minutes are gone whether you
finish or not. Two minutes on three fresh questions has a better expected return
than two minutes on one you may still not land.
*Share reason: every single person has done this and knows they'd do it again.
It's a confession disguised as a question.*

### B2. The eliminated options
Negative marking is −1. You can confidently eliminate 2 of the 4 options.

**Guess / Skip**

→ **Guess.** Two options left means a 50% shot: +3 half the time, −1 the other
half, so you gain on average. The rule worth keeping: eliminate two, always
guess. Eliminate none, never guess.
*Share reason: settles a genuine argument that runs in every CAT group.*

### B3. Where do the last 3 minutes go?
Three minutes left. You can either review 4 answers you were unsure about, or
attempt 2 questions you haven't read yet.

**Review / Attempt**

→ **Attempt the new ones.** Reviewing usually changes an answer you had a 50%
chance on to another you have a 50% chance on. Two unread questions are two
fresh chances at full marks.
*Share reason: most people do the opposite, and will want to argue.*

### B4. The dip
Your mock percentile went 85 → 78. What do you do first?

- A) Change your whole strategy
- B) Take another mock immediately
- **C) Analyse this one properly**
- D) Go back to revising basics

→ **C.** One mock is a data point, not a trend, and you cannot fix what you
haven't diagnosed. The most expensive habit in CAT prep is taking mock after
mock without reading any of them.
*Share reason: the student who just had a bad mock sends it to the friend who
also just had one.*

### B5. Two passages
Two RCs left. One is about a topic you genuinely find interesting. One is dry
and about economics.

**Interesting first / Dry first**

→ **Interesting first**, but not for the reason most people think. It isn't
about enjoyment — you read faster and retain more on familiar ground, so it
costs fewer minutes for the same marks. Save the expensive one for when you know
exactly how much time it can have.
*Share reason: it validates something people already do but feel guilty about.*

### B6. Accuracy or attempts?
You're at 85% accuracy but attempting only 60% of the section.

**Attempt more / Protect accuracy**

→ **Attempt more.** 85% accuracy usually means you're skipping questions you
could have got. Very high accuracy is often a sign of over-caution, not skill.
*Share reason: contradicts what most students are told, so it starts a debate.*

### B7. The first ten minutes
You open the QA section. What do you do in the first 60 seconds?

- A) Start question 1
- **B) Scan the whole section quickly**
- C) Start with your strongest topic
- D) Read the instructions

→ **B.** One minute of scanning tells you where the easy marks are. Starting at
question 1 assumes the paper is ordered by difficulty, and it never is.
*Share reason: a genuinely actionable habit that takes zero effort to adopt.*

### B8. Which set would you leave?
Two DILR sets remain, 15 minutes left.

- **Set A:** you understand it completely but it needs a big table.
- **Set B:** you're not sure you understand it, but if you do it's quick.

**A / B**

→ **A.** "I understand it and it's laborious" is a known cost. "I might
understand it" is an unknown one, and unknown costs are what destroy DILR
sections. Take the boring guaranteed set.
*Share reason: this is the exact decision people lose sleep over.*

### B9. The rewrite
You've been studying 5 hours a day for two weeks and your mocks haven't moved.

- A) Study 7 hours
- B) Change the material you're using
- **C) Look at what those 5 hours actually contain**
- D) Take a break

→ **C.** Five hours of re-reading things you already know is not five hours of
preparation. Effort is easy to measure and easy to fake; difficulty is the only
thing that moves a score.
*Share reason: uncomfortably true, and everybody knows someone it describes.*

### B10. Would you attempt this?
*"A shopkeeper has 3 types of tea. He mixes them in a ratio such that the
resulting blend, sold at a 20% profit, yields the same profit as selling type A
alone at 35%..."*

**Attempt / Skip**

→ **Skip.** You don't even need to finish reading it. Three variables, a
conditional, and a comparison — that's a 4-minute question wearing a 1-minute
costume. Recognising the shape is worth more marks than solving it.
*Share reason: the relief of being told it's fine to walk away.*

# 🧠 Gotcha

Easy arithmetic, wrong instinct. Every one of these is answerable in under a
minute — the trap is in the reading, never in the maths.

### B11. The bat and the ball
A bat and a ball cost ₹110 together. The bat costs ₹100 more than the ball.
How much is the ball?

- A) ₹10 · **B) ₹5** · C) ₹15 · D) ₹11

→ **₹5.** If the ball were ₹10, the bat would be ₹110 and the total ₹120. Ball
₹5, bat ₹105, difference exactly ₹100.
*Share reason: the single most shareable question in this document. Almost
everyone says ₹10 instantly, and the sender knows it.*

### B12. The overtake
You're running a race and you overtake the person in **second place**. What
position are you in now?

- A) 1st · **B) 2nd** · C) 3rd · D) can't say

→ **2nd.** You took the place of the person you passed. To be first you'd have
to overtake the person in first.
*Share reason: pure "I can't believe I got that wrong", zero maths, works on
non-CAT friends too.*

### B13. The machines
5 machines make 5 widgets in 5 minutes. How long do 100 machines take to make
100 widgets?

- A) 100 min · **B) 5 min** · C) 20 min · D) 1 min

→ **5 minutes.** One machine makes one widget in 5 minutes. Scaling both sides
equally changes nothing.
*Share reason: the wrong answer (100) is so confident that the correction lands
hard.*

### B14. The lily pad
A patch of lilies doubles in size every day and covers the whole lake on day 48.
On which day was the lake half covered?

- A) day 24 · **B) day 47** · C) day 46 · D) day 12

→ **Day 47.** It doubles, so the day before full is exactly half.
*Share reason: genuinely beautiful, and it's the clearest illustration of
compounding anyone will see this week.*

### B15. Which discount?
A ₹1,000 jacket. Which is better for you?

- A) 20% off, then a 10% coupon
- **B) A flat 30% off**
- C) Identical
- D) Depends on the order

→ **B.** A: 1000 × 0.8 × 0.9 = **₹720**. B: **₹700**. Stacked discounts always
come out worse than their sum, because the second one applies to a smaller
amount.
*Share reason: it's about shopping, not CAT. That's exactly why it travels.*

### B16. Does the order matter?
A price goes up 50% and then down 50%. Another goes down 50% and then up 50%.
Which ends higher?

- A) The first · B) The second · **C) They're equal** · D) Depends on the start

→ **Equal** — both land at 75% of the original. Multiplication doesn't care
about order. And notice neither one returns to where it started.
*Share reason: two surprises stacked in one line, and the second one is the
useful one.*

### B17. The cats
3 cats catch 3 mice in 3 minutes. How many cats to catch 100 mice in 100
minutes?

- A) 100 · **B) 3** · C) 33 · D) 30

→ **3.** One cat catches one mouse in 3 minutes, so in 100 minutes one cat
catches about 33 — and three cats get you to 100.
*Share reason: same family as the machines, different costume, and people who
got the machines right still trip on this one.*

### B18. Half price, twice
A phone's price is cut by 50%, then cut by 50% again. Total discount?

- A) 100% · **B) 75%** · C) 50% · D) 25%

→ **75%.** ₹1000 → ₹500 → ₹250. Two halvings never reach zero — each one is
smaller than the last.
*Share reason: the "100%" instinct is strong and instantly, obviously wrong once
stated.*

### B19. Filling the tank
A tap fills a tank in 6 hours. A second tap fills it in 3 hours. Both together?

- A) 4.5 hrs · **B) 2 hrs** · C) 9 hrs · D) 1.5 hrs

→ **2 hours.** Rates add: 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2. And sanity-check the instinct —
together must be faster than the *faster* tap alone, so any answer above 3 hours
was wrong before you calculated.
*Share reason: the sanity-check is the actual gift here, and it generalises.*

# 🕵️ Spot it

### B20. The odd number
Which does not belong: **4, 9, 16, 24, 25**

- A) 4 · B) 9 · **C) 24** · D) 25

→ **24** — the only one that isn't a perfect square.
*Share reason: fast, clean, and satisfying to get in three seconds.*

### B21. Fact or opinion
Which of these is an opinion?

- A) The report was published in March.
- B) The committee had nine members.
- **C) The delay was unnecessary.**
- D) The vote was 6–3.

→ **C.** "Unnecessary" is a judgement; the rest can be checked. Half of VARC is
noticing which sentences can be verified and which are someone's view.
*Share reason: it's a skill people are quietly proud of having.*

### B22. Two truths
Three students. Exactly one is telling the truth.
A: "I finished the mock." B: "A is lying." C: "B is lying."

Who is telling the truth?

- A) A · **B) B** · C) C · D) impossible

→ **B.** If B is truthful, A lied and C lied (because C says B lies, which is
false). Exactly one truth-teller — consistent. Test the other two and both break.
*Share reason: puzzle-shaped, not exam-shaped. People send puzzles.*

### B23. What's actually claimed
*"Students who use flashcards score higher on average."*

Which is definitely true?

- A) Flashcards improve scores.
- **B) Flashcard users have a higher average score.**
- C) You should use flashcards.
- D) Non-users score badly.

→ **B** — the only option that repeats the sentence rather than extending it.
The other three are all conclusions the sentence never reached.
*Share reason: once you see this pattern you see it in every headline, which is
a genuinely useful thing to have been given.*

# ⚡ One-minute shortcut

### B24. 99 × 47
Without a calculator, in under ten seconds:

- A) 4,553 · **B) 4,653** · C) 4,753 · D) 4,663

→ **4,653.** 99 × 47 = (100 × 47) − 47 = 4700 − 47. Any multiplication by 9s,
99s or 999s becomes a subtraction.
*Share reason: an immediately reusable trick — people share things they'll
actually use tomorrow.*

### B25. 35 squared
- A) 1,125 · **B) 1,225** · C) 1,025 · D) 1,325

→ **1,225.** Any number ending in 5: take the first digit(s), multiply by the
next integer up (3 × 4 = 12), write 25 after it. 65² = 6×7 then 25 = 4,225.
*Share reason: feels like a magic trick and works every time.*

### B26. 15% of 240
- A) 32 · **B) 36** · C) 34 · D) 40

→ **36.** 10% is 24, half of that is 12, add them. Every percentage is 10% plus
halves — no formula needed.
*Share reason: the method is more useful than the answer, and it works on
restaurant bills.*

### B27. Adding 1 to 100
- A) 4,950 · **B) 5,050** · C) 5,000 · D) 5,100

→ **5,050.** Pair them: 1+100, 2+99, 3+98 … fifty pairs of 101.
*Share reason: the pairing idea is the kind of thing people enjoy explaining to
someone else, which is the purest form of sharing.*
